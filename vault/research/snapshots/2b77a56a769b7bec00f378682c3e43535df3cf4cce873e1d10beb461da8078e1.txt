# Performance Comparison of HTTP/3 and HTTP/2: Proxy vs. Non-Proxy Environments

###### Abstract.

This paper systematically evaluates the performance of QUIC/HTTP3 (H3) and TCP/HTTP2 (H2) protocols in proxy-enhanced environments. H3 leverages features such as UDP-based flow-controlled streams, integrated TLS, multiplexed connections, and connection migration, offering the potential for improved web communication. Despite extensive research, the impact of proxy integration and connection migration remains underexplored. This study addresses this gap by evaluating H3 and H2 across various scenarios, particularly in noisy networks and proxy setups. Our findings show that H3 excels under high loss and high latency conditions, significantly benefiting from its connection migration and multiplexing features, with improvements of up to 88.36% under high-loss and high-latency conditions, and 81.5% under extreme loss conditions, respectively. H3’s connection migration remains robust, maintaining stable performance even in proxy-enhanced environments, ensuring seamless network transitions. The proxy has a more neutral impact on H3, while it significantly enhances H2 performance, particularly when paired with BBR, resulting in a 90% improvement in the single-stream file download experiment under severe network impairments. Any improvements observed in H3 under a proxy are minor and do not fundamentally alter H3’s performance as they do for H2. Importantly, while H2 with the right congestion control algorithm (CCA) can achieve performance comparable to H3, H3’s performance is more robust, as it is less impacted by network conditions, proxy settings, and CCA variations.

###### Keywords:

QUIC, HTTP, Multiplexing, Connection Migration, Proxy, Web Performance## 1. Introduction

The rapid evolution of network technologies, driven by the increasing demands of internet-based services, has led to significant advancements in communication protocols and network architecture. Among these advancements, the development of QUIC ([Iyengar and Thomson, 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib40)) and HTTP/3 ([Bishop, 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib27)) significantly improve the speed, reliability, and security of web communications. QUIC, initially developed by Google and later standardized by the IETF([Wik, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib3)), serves as the foundation for HTTP/3 and has the potential to reshape the Internet and its applications and services.

QUIC introduces several innovative features that distinguish it from other previous protocols. It enables faster connection establishment and supports multiplexed connections without head-of-line (HoL) blocking, a substantial limitation in TCP. Furthermore, QUIC uniquely supports connection migration, which allows ongoing sessions to continue even when the client’s network endpoint changes (e.g., when switching from Wi-Fi to cellular networks) ([Iyengar and Thomson, 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib40)). HTTP/3 builds on QUIC by incorporating these advancements to streamline data transmission processes, enhancing web page load times and responsiveness through smarter packet processing and prioritization of requests ([Bishop, 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib27)).

Major companies such as Google ([chr, 2013](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib2)), Akamai ([Bishop, 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib26)), Meta ([Joras and Chi, 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib42)), Microsoft ([Banks, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib24)), and Cloudflare ([Ghedini and Lalkaka, 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib37)) have adopted QUIC commercially, indicating its wide adoption and significant impact on the global web infrastructure. As of April 1, 2024, 71.4% of desktop browsers and 70.8% of mobile browsers support HTTP/3 among the top 1000 websites ([htt, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib21)), underscoring its growing relevance.

Despite prior research exploring the performance of QUIC ([Ganji and Shahzad, 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib36); [Kakhki et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib43); [Marx et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib53); [Piraux et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib61); [Shreedhar et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib68); [Wolsing et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib78); [Yu and Benson, 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib80); [Trevisan et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib73); [Jaeger et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib41); [Rüth et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib65); [Wang et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib75); [Liu and Crowley, 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib50); [Nepomuceno et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib58); [Bhat et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib25)), a critical gap remains in understanding how these protocols perform in the presence of proxies. Proxies are ubiquitous in modern web architectures, serving essential roles in content delivery, load balancing, and security. However, the interaction between these next-generation protocols and proxies is still underexplored, particularly in terms of their impact on connection migration, performance in noisy networks, and overall user experience.

This work aims to address these gaps by providing a comprehensive performance comparison of TCP/HTTP2 (H2) and QUIC/HTTP3 (H3) in both proxy and non-proxy environments, focusing on how these protocols behave under real-world impairments such as packet loss and network delay. Specifically, we measure key performance metrics, including total request time and visual metrics like Speed Index ([spe, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib20)), to characterize the performance of these protocols across various use cases such as single-stream, multi-stream, connection migration, and web page loading. These measurements are critical to understanding how H2 and H3 impact web performance and user-perceived experience, particularly in complex network environments involving proxies.

This paper offers several novel contributions to the understanding of H2 and H3 performance in diverse network environments, with a particular emphasis on proxy usage and connection migration:

Comprehensive Performance Evaluation with Proxy Integration: Unlike prior work, which predominantly focuses on direct connections, this paper provides the first thorough comparative analysis of H2 and H3 performance in both proxy-enhanced and non-proxy environments. Our results show that while proxies significantly enhance H2’s performance, especially when paired with the right congestion control algorithm (e.g., BBR), their impact on H3 is more neutral due to QUIC’s inherent features. This comparison sheds light on the practical implications for protocol selection in real-world proxy-based networks.

First-of-its-Kind Analysis of Connection Migration under Proxy Conditions: To the best of our knowledge, this paper is the first to evaluate QUIC’s connection migration capabilities under proxy conditions, including scenarios involving IP address changes and network impairments. Our results demonstrate H3’s ability to maintain low request time and ensure seamless user experience during network transitions, highlighting its suitability for mobile and dynamic networking environments.

Experimentation with Congestion Control Algorithms (CCAs) for H2 and H3: Through experiments with multiple CCAs (e.g., BBR, Cubic, NewReno), this study provides insights into how different CCAs affect H2 and H3 performance. We highlight conditions where BBR notably improves H2’s performance, while demonstrating that H3’s internal congestion control mechanisms ensure consistent performance across various network conditions, making it a reliable choice for broader use cases.

All our study data and scripts are available on GitHub.

## 2. Related Work

Performance of QUIC
Extensive research has explored QUIC’s enhancements over traditional protocols, emphasizing its ability to significantly improve web browsing experiences. Early investigations by Carlucci et al. ([Carlucci et al., 2015](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib28)) demonstrated QUIC’s superior performance over TCP and SPDY ([Wang et al., 2014](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib76)), particularly in terms of connection setup time and robustness to packet loss. Cunha et al. ([Da Cunha et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib31)) further highlighted the conditions in which QUIC excels and when it underperforms. QUIC’s evolution has led to efforts investigating interoperability across implementations ([Jaeger et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib41); [Marx et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib53); [Seemann and Iyengar, 2020](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib67)). Long-term studies by Kakhki et al. ([Kakhki et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib43)) and Piraux et al. ([Piraux et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib61)) traced QUIC’s progression over time. Additionally, Rüth et al. ([Rüth et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib64)) examined QUIC deployment and usage in its early stages, while QScanner ([Zirngibl et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib83)) analyzed early QUIC deployments.

Research has also explored QUIC’s impact on various applications. For video streaming, studies by Palmer et al. ([Palmer et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib60)), Paiva et al. ([do Prado Paiva et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib33)), Zheng et al. ([Zheng et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib82)), Shreedhar et al. ([Shreedhar et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib68)), and Xu et al. ([Xu et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib79)) have highlighted QUIC’s benefits. In web browsing, research by Das et al. ([Das, 2014](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib32)), Rüth et al. ([Rüth et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib65)), Wolsing et al. ([Wolsing et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib78)), and Shreedhar et al. ([Shreedhar et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib68)) have shown how QUIC can enhance user experiences. Studies on mixed workloads by Shreedhar et al. ([Shreedhar et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib68)) and Wolsing et al. ([Wolsing et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib78)) extend these findings. Additionally, research has evaluated QUIC’s performance on different platforms, including mobile environments ([Ganji and Shahzad, 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib36)) and satellite communications ([Endres et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib34); [Kosek et al., 2022b](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib46)). Siag et al. ([Siag et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib69)) also developed a predictive model for QUIC’s performance. Kempf et al. ([Kempf et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib44)) presents an extension to the QUIC Interop Runner for testing the interoperability of QUIC implementations.

Features of QUIC
Research on HTTP/2 and HTTP/3, such as the studies by Marx et al. ([Marx et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib52)) and Sander et al. ([Sander et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib66)), has provided in-depth analysis of how resource multiplexing and prioritization affect web performance. These studies highlight that while H3’s use of QUIC reduces HoL blocking compared to H2, real-world gains depend heavily on effective prioritization strategies. Other research has extended QUIC to multipath QUIC (MPQUIC) ([Coninck and Bonaventure, 2017](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib29); [Ingenzi et al., [n. d.]](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib39)), and new packet schedulers for MPQUIC ([do Prado Paiva et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib33); [Rabitsch et al., 2018](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib62); [Wang et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib74)) and upper-layer solutions ([Zheng et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib82)) have followed. MultipathTester ([Coninck and Bonaventure, 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib30)) compares the performance of MPTCP with MPQUIC on iOS. Several studies have focused on QUIC congestion control ([Engelbart and Ott, 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib35); [Haile et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib38); [Mishra et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib54)), while others examine security and privacy ([Kosek et al., 2022c](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib47); [Lychev et al., 2015](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib51); [Nawrocki et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib56); [Nawrocki et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib57); [Soni and Rajput, 2020](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib70)). Experimental protocols inspired by QUIC, like TCPLS ([Rochet et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib63)), DCQUIC ([Tan et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib72)), and MCQUIC ([Navarre et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib55)), further show its potential.

Connection Migration in QUIC
Despite the importance of connection migration in QUIC for mobile and transitioning networks, detailed analyses focused on this feature in diverse networking contexts are scarce. Google has demonstrated the benefits of connection migration in a demo video ([goo, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib17)), but empirical studies remain limited. Tan et al. ([Tan et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib71)) discuss how QUIC uses connection migration to uniquely identify connections, proposing a Proactive Connection Migration (PCM) mechanism to optimize network selection in heterogeneous environments.

QUIC with Proxy
Exploring QUIC in proxy architectures is relatively sparse, but some studies have started to bridge this gap. Kosek et al. ([Kosek et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib48)) introduced Secure Middlebox-Assisted QUIC (SMAQ), enabling middleboxes in QUIC connections while preserving security, and demonstrated performance improvements in satellite networks. Another study by Kosek et al. ([Kosek et al., 2022a](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib45)) evaluated QUIC and HTTP/3 with and without performance-enhancing proxies (PEPs) in satellite networks, showing significant performance gains, especially in high-latency conditions. Kühlewind et al. ([Kühlewind et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib49)) assessed the QUIC-based MASQUE proxying protocol for tunneling UDP and IP traffic, revealing benefits like local recovery in lossy links despite introducing overhead. However, these studies focus on different aspects of QUIC and proxies. Our work uniquely compares H2 and H3 in proxy-enhanced environments, with a particular emphasis on QUIC’s connection migration feature, evaluating the performance impacts of proxy and connection migration under various network conditions.

Research Gap and Study Focus This paper aims to fill these gaps by systematically examining QUIC and HTTP/3’s performance across single-stream, multi-stream, connection migration, and web page loading scenarios. By focusing on the fine-grained performance aspects of QUIC and HTTP/3, especially the under-researched area of connection migration within complex architectures, this study provides nuanced insights that could guide future optimizations and implementations of these protocols in networks containing proxies. This research not only fills a knowledge gap but provides experimental design and software for future studies to explore the scalability and robustness of QUIC and HTTP/3 in dynamic and varied network settings.

## 3. Experimental Setup

We use a hardware network test bed that is remotely accessible for networking researchers and educators ([Wiseman et al., 2008](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib77)). The client and proxy machines run Ubuntu 20.04.6 LTS on Intel(R) Xeon(R) E5520 CPUs with 16GB RAM, while the server runs Ubuntu 22.04.4 LTS on an Intel(R) Core(TM) i3-3240 CPU with 16GB RAM. These machines are interconnected through a router via a 1 Gbps Ethernet network. The ’tc netem’ utility ([tc, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib22)) is used for bandwidth control, packet loss, and delay manipulation. In all experiments, the bandwidth is set to 10 Mbps, with specific loss and delay parameters detailed in each experiment’s setup (see Table [1](https://arxiv.org#S2.T1)). We use a fixed bandwidth to maintain controlled conditions, allowing us to isolate the effects of proxy environments on the performance of H2 and H3. These bandwidth limitations, combined with varied loss and delay parameters on both uplink and downlink, represent realistic edge-case network conditions that induce unique protocol behaviors for root cause analysis. The study by Zhang et al. ([Zhang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib81)) has already explored performance under varying bandwidth, while both our work and studies like ([Yu and Benson, 2021](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib80)) use fixed bandwidth with varied loss and delay to focus specifically on how network impairments affect protocol performance, without introducing the additional complexity of fluctuating bandwidth.

We use Nginx ([ngi, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib12)) as the server and Envoy Proxy ([env, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib8)) as the reverse proxy, both widely adopted in practice. Six clients are employed: Proxygen ([pro, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib15)), Curl ([cur, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib7)), Chrome ([chr, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib5)), Wget ([wge, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib23)), Quiche ([qui, 2024a](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib18)), and aioquic ([aio, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib4)), ensuring a representative sample. The selection of clients, server configurations, and versions is detailed in Appendix A.1 Table [2](https://arxiv.org#A1.T2), informed by the QUIC workgroup’s implementation list ([qui, 2024b](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib19)). Our criteria included support for HTTP/3, adherence to QUIC version 1, open-source availability, and popularity based on GitHub stars. Moreover, we ensured diversity by including implementations in different programming languages, aiming to reflect a broad spectrum of real-world applications.

In each experiment, we conducted 10 runs. We use Total Request Time as the metric in single-stream, multi-stream, and connection migration experiments, measuring from the client’s first request packet to the server until the client closes the connection. For web page loading, we use the Speed Index ([spe, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib20)), a metric that measures how quickly content appears on the user’s screen.

### 3.1. Baseline Experiment Setup

This experiment setup establishes the baseline for evaluating the performance of H2 and H3 in single-stream request, multi-stream request, connection migration, and web page loading. We employ Cubic as the CCA on the client side, and New Reno on the server side for both QUIC and TCP. Cubic is the default CCA for most H3 clients and the default CCA on Linux. For QUIC, the New Reno is the only CCA that Nginx supports. Both H2 and H3 use TLS 1.3. Our experimental setup is detailed in Table [1](https://arxiv.org#S2.T1), with the logical topology illustrated in Figure [1](https://arxiv.org#acmlabel1).

The workloads for single-stream, multi-stream, and connection migration are random plaintext generated by the Faker library ([fak, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib9)). For web page loading, we followed the methodology of Marx et al. ([Marx et al., 2019](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib52)) and Sander et al. ([Sander et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib66)), selecting 18 popular websites with varying numbers of resources and sizes shown in Figure [2](https://arxiv.org#acmlabel2). The websites were downloaded using Wget ([wge, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib23)) and hosted locally without external links on the Nginx server to eliminate network variability.

In the connection migration experiment, We extended Cloudflare’s Rust-based quiche client to support connection migration. Specifically, we took inspiration from the design of Andriod’s Connection Manager ([Con, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib6)) to detect changed interfaces/IP addresses, and initiate the connection migration process as described in ([Iyengar and Thomson, 2022](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib40)). For H2, we employed wget with and without range Requests ([ran, 2024](https://arxiv.org#bib.bib10)), along with curl tests without range requests. Range requests in H2 facilitate fetching a range of data. This allows us to explore H2’s ability to pause and resume downloading in the change in connection scenario. The experiment simulates IP address change after downloading over 2MB of a 5MB test file to mimic real-world network transitions, evaluating how each protocol maintains connection stability and download continuity during these transitions.

### 3.2. Proxy Experiment Setup

This experiment setup assesses the impact of introducing an Envoy reverse proxy between the server and client, with loss and delay applied between the proxy and the client. The reverse proxy breaks the connection between the client and the server and establishes a new connection with the server. We use BBR as the CCA for both QUIC and TCP since it is the default CCA for QUIC on Envoy. We disabled the proxy cache and retry features. The connection bandwidth is uniformly set at 10 Mbps across all links. We chose Envoy for its robust, production-grade capabilities and widespread adoption by major companies like Google, Microsoft, and Amazon. The network logical topology positions the Envoy between the client and server, as detailed in the accompanying Figure [3](https://arxiv.org#acmlabel3).

## 4. Experimental Evaluation

In this section, we present the results of our experiments and our analysis of the results.

### 4.1. Single-Stream Experiment Evaluation

Figure [4](https://arxiv.org#acmlabel4) presents the results of our single-stream experiments across various network conditions, highlighting the performance differences between H2 and H3.

Ideal Network Conditions
Under ideal network conditions (10 Mbps bandwidth, 0% packet loss, 0 ms delay), both H2 and H3 exhibited similar performance, taking approximately 9 seconds to download a 10 MB file (see Figure [4(a)](https://arxiv.org#S3.F4.sf1)). The introduction of a proxy resulted in a minor increase in download time for both protocols, adding roughly 0-1 seconds. This proxy overhead was slightly more pronounced for H3 than for H2, likely due to Envoy Proxy’s experimental support for H3, compared to its mature, optimized support for H2.

Moderate Network Impairments
In scenarios involving moderate impairments (10 Mbps bandwidth, 0% packet loss, and 50 ms delay), both H2 and H3 demonstrated comparable performance without the use of a proxy (Figure [4(b)](https://arxiv.org#S3.F4.sf2)). However, The introduction of a proxy caused slight variations in performance for H3 clients, such as curl and quiche, but overall, H3 maintained consistent performance with minimal impact from the proxy. In contrast, H2’s performance remained stable across different clients, suggesting that H2 implementations are more mature and exhibit fewer discrepancies between implementations. In scenarios with loss only (10 Mbps bandwidth, 2-4% packet loss, 0 ms delay), both H2 and H3 exhibited comparable performance.

Severe Network Impairments
Under severe network conditions (10 Mbps bandwidth, 4% packet loss, and 50 ms delay), the performance differences between H2 and H3 became more pronounced (Figure [4(c)](https://arxiv.org#S3.F4.sf3)). H3 clients were able to complete the 10 MB file download in about 15 seconds, whereas H2 clients took approximately 106 seconds without a proxy. The introduction of a proxy using the BBR CCA significantly improved H2’s performance, reducing download times to around 10 seconds, representing a 90% improvement. Notably, other CCAs like Cubic and New Reno did not show this enhancement for H2, underlining the importance of CCA selection in impaired networks. However, it’s crucial to note that H2’s performance remains highly dependent on the chosen CCA, unlike H3, which is less impacted by such variations. H3’s performance remained consistent regardless of the CCA used, indicating that QUIC’s internal congestion control mechanisms are robust under such conditions.
